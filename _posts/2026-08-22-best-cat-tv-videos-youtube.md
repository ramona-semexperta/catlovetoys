If you've ever handed your phone to your cat — intentionally or not — and watched them stare at it, wide-eyed and totally still, for far longer than seems reasonable, you already know "cat TV" is a real phenomenon. It's not just a gimmick. For a lot of cats, the right kind of video content taps directly into the same visual triggers that make real prey irresistible.

## Why It Works

Cats are wired to react to specific visual patterns: small size, erratic movement, quick direction changes, and a certain kind of scurrying, low-to-the-ground motion. Real insects and rodents move this way — and so, conveniently, do a lot of simple screen recordings of bugs and birds. The brain doesn't fully distinguish "small thing skittering across a flat surface" from "small thing skittering across a screen." The instinct fires either way.

This is exactly what happened with our own Alfi. She had one video in particular — a simple recording of cockroaches scurrying around — that she treated like appointment television. If you were in bed and hadn't already put it on for her, she'd come find your phone and make it extremely clear that showtime was overdue. Once it started, she'd watch, almost hypnotized, tracking every scurry across the screen for as long as it played.

## A Word on Screen Time vs. Real Play

Cat TV is a great supplement, but it shouldn't replace physical play entirely. Watching a screen can hold attention and provide mental stimulation, but it doesn't offer the same payoff as a real toy — there's no actual "catch" moment at the end, no physical exertion, and no interaction with you. Think of it as a good option for moments you can't run an active play session (working at your desk, winding down before bed), not a full substitute for daily wand-toy time.

## Videos Worth Trying

If you want to test whether your cat is a "screen cat," a few starting points:

- **[The exact cockroach video Alfi loved](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EOK86dDB0O8)** — simple, close-up footage of cockroaches scurrying, which is exactly the kind of erratic, low-to-the-ground movement that tends to grab a cat's attention
- General search terms worth trying on YouTube if that one doesn't land for your cat: "cat TV bird feeder," "cat games mouse on screen," "fish tank video for cats," and "bug video for cats" — there's a whole genre of these, and different cats respond to different "prey" types
- Some cats respond best to bird and squirrel footage, others to bugs, others to fish — it's worth trying a couple of different types before deciding your cat "isn't a screen cat"

## Tips for Trying It With Your Own Cat

- **Volume matters.** Some cats respond more to videos with real ambient sound (rustling, scurrying) than silent footage.
- **Screen size and distance matter too.** A phone held close, or a tablet propped at floor level, tends to work better than a TV across the room — cats respond more to something at their scale and eye level.
- **Don't force it.** Some cats have zero interest in screens no matter what's on them, and that's completely normal — it's a bonus enrichment option, not a requirement.
- **Keep an eye on the paws.** A cat that gets really worked up might start pawing at the screen — worth keeping devices secure so an excited swipe doesn't send a phone off a nightstand.

## The Takeaway

Cat TV isn't just a cute video trend — it's a legitimate (if partial) form of enrichment that works because it hijacks the same visual instincts a real hunt would. It's never going to replace a proper play session with a toy you control, but for a cat who gets genuinely locked in, it's an easy, free way to add a bit more stimulation to a quiet afternoon or a wind-down evening.
