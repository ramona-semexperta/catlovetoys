There's a running joke that cats are happier with the box a toy arrived in than the toy itself. It's not really a joke — it's mostly true, and it points to something useful: cats don't need expensive or elaborate objects to engage in real, instinct-satisfying play. A lot of the best "toys" are things already sitting around most homes.

Here are a few simple ones worth trying before your next online order.

## 1. The Paper Bag Ambush Tunnel

Lay a paper grocery bag (handles removed, no plastic ones — those can be dangerous) on its side. That's it. The rustling sound, the enclosed hiding space, and the "ambush" setup tap directly into a cat's instinct to stalk from cover. Cutting a second hole in the opposite end turns it into a tunnel cats can dart through, which adds a chase element too.

## 2. The Cardboard Box Puzzle Feeder

Cut several holes of different sizes into an empty shoebox or similar sturdy box, just big enough for a paw to fit through. Drop a few treats or kibble inside. Your cat has to work — reaching, patting, sometimes tipping the box — to get the reward out, which is a lot closer to natural foraging behavior than food appearing instantly in a bowl.

## 3. The Sock Kicker

Take an old sock, add a small amount of dried catnip (a teaspoon is usually plenty), tie a secure knot, and you have a kicker toy cats can grab with their front paws and "bunny kick" with their back legs — a very natural predatory motion. Just make sure the knot is tight and check periodically for wear, since a torn sock isn't something you want your cat ingesting.

## 4. The DIY Wand Toy

Tie a strip of fabric, a feather, or a bit of crumpled paper to the end of a chopstick, pencil, or dowel using string. This recreates the exact function of a store-bought teaser wand: you control unpredictable movement, your cat gets to stalk and pounce. Always supervise string-based toys and put them away after play — loose string left out is a real ingestion hazard.

## 5. The Ice Cube Chase (Warm Weather Only)

On a hot day, dropping an ice cube on a hard floor (tile or hardwood, not carpet) gives some cats an irresistible, unpredictably sliding target to chase. Not every cat is into this one, but for water-curious or high-energy cats it's an easy zero-cost option worth trying once.

## 6. The T-Shirt Hanger Tag

This one comes from personal experience: our cat Alfi's favorite toy in her entire life wasn't anything we bought — it was the small fabric hanger loop cut off the collar of a t-shirt or top. Nearly every piece of clothing has one, and once it's cut free, it's just the right size and texture for a cat to hook with their claws and drag around like prey. She went back to it for years, no batteries, no packaging, no cost at all.

## 7. The Crinkle Ball

Crumple a piece of scrap paper into a tight ball. The crinkling sound alone triggers curiosity in a lot of cats, and a paper ball is light enough to bat around and chase across a floor the same way a store-bought crinkle toy would.

## A Few Safety Notes

DIY toys are great for cost and immediacy, but they do need more supervision than store-bought toys, since they're not designed and tested specifically for pet safety:

- Remove and discard anything showing wear, tearing, or loose small pieces
- Never leave string, ribbon, or yarn out unsupervised — ingestion risk is real and can require emergency surgery
- Avoid rubber bands, bottle caps, or anything small enough to swallow whole
- Supervise ice cube and water-based play so floors don't become a slipping hazard

## The Takeaway

Some of the most effective play doesn't come from a package at all — it comes from tapping into the same instincts a $20 toy is trying to trigger, using things already in a kitchen drawer. It's a good reminder that "engaging" and "expensive" aren't the same thing, even if store-bought toys still have a real place in a well-rounded toy rotation.
